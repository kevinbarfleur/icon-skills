# Product meaning into a composition

The noun in a brief is usually the category, not the idea.
A note app might offer relief from holding a thought; a planning app might preserve an opening rather than display another calendar.
Describe the change before choosing the objects.

A useful direction sentence has three parts: the user's change, a visible action or tension, and the intended feeling.
For example, a scheduling service could reserve one generous opening in a rhythm of occupied spaces.
The empty interval does the work; an extra clock does not necessarily help.
This is a reasoning example, not a mark to reuse for every organizational app.

## Distinct hypotheses

Change the visual grammar between alternatives: solid versus line, singular versus relational, stable versus directional, organic versus constructed, positive mass versus negative space.
Different colors on the same arrangement are variants, not different ideas.
Do not force all these contrasts into every brief.
A few rough SVG silhouettes can reveal whether the alternatives differ before lighting and surface treatment consume attention.

Choose one main visual event.
Secondary elements should make that event clearer, establish scale or explain contact.
If a detail exists only because the image seems empty, test removing it.
Absence can carry meaning.

## Color and material are decisions

Use the [color direction review](color-direction.md) on the actual composition.
Changing hex values is not enough if every product retains the same muted field, pale mass and warm accent.
Color direction is part of choosing the identity, not a finishing preset.

Flat geometry can have more authority than a simulated object.
Use depth when it makes contact, overlap, tension or material intelligible.
A gradient without a lighting or material reason is decoration, not polish.
Grain, gloss, shadows and rounded shapes are optional, not completion criteria.

## Originality relative to what is visible

State what distinguishes a candidate from the examples and other candidates in its silhouette, relationship or visual action.
This is a comparison with known work, not a claim of worldwide originality.
A shared stock icon remains non-exclusive.
Read [collection decisions](decisions.md) for examples where repeated constructions weakened distinctiveness.

## Choose before polishing

Put the rough candidates side by side and temporarily hide their explanations.
Describe only what is actually visible, then compare that description with the intended product change.
If the explanation does all the work, either strengthen the visible relationship or admit that this is a category symbol rather than a specific product idea.
Category recognition can be useful, but it is not sufficient evidence of an intentional identity.

In a fresh-project trial, a well-drawn greenhouse communicated gardening but the intended handover between volunteers remained invisible.
In another trial, a smooth open band with a separate dot was explained as available money and a protected reserve, but mainly looked like a letter C.
These were usable SVGs, not strong evidence of distinctive product direction.
Do not fix a weak idea by adding more decorative parts or a more persuasive description.
Reconsider the contour, counterform, interaction or selected hypothesis before spending another pass on tiny defects.

A minimal mark can be the strongest option when its proportions, gesture or negative space have a specific character.
There is no detail quota and no requirement to encode the whole feature set.
The goal is a compelling visible decision that survives without the pitch, not a literal diagram of the service.
